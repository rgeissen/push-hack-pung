Performance · Energy · Measured on a Push 3

Silence costs almost nothing. Playing costs only the sound.

A Push 3 Standalone has four processor cores and a battery, and Live needs its share of both. Pung treats them as one budget. One layer, the governor, measures what every part of Pung costs and decides from those numbers: what may stop while nothing sounds, what may start, and how often anything is drawn.

The idea

One budget

Performance and energy are the same question asked at two moments. While you play, every core Pung leaves free is headroom for Live: more tracks, more devices, no dropout. While you do not, every core Pung leaves alone is battery, and a cooler Push.

So Pung has one layer for both, not a performance layer and an energy layer side by side. It works from measurements taken on the Push itself, not from estimates. The numbers on this page are percent of one processor core; the Push has four, so 400 is everything it has.

Architecture

The governor

The governor has two halves. One measures: what each device's sound thread takes, each emulator, Pung's own view and web page, Live, and what is left. The other decides, and it decides only four things.

Energy

Emulators rest in silence

Pung's four native instruments emulate a hardware synth's processors, and an emulated processor works as hard silent as playing. That was the largest cost Pung had, and nobody heard anything for it.

Each emulator runs in a process of its own, and its device decides, block by block, whether it can be heard: the output silent for a whole block, no key held, nothing sent to the synth for ten seconds, nothing waiting to be sent. Then the device stops reading the emulator's audio and answers silence, the emulator finds its buffer full and sleeps, and the processor takes nothing. A note, a knob or a new sound wakes it before the message goes in.

JE-8086 and Osirus are Schwung's emulators, written for Move, where Schwung's own host stops asking a silent module for sound. Pung does the same for these two after the same ten seconds, and JE-8086's three emulator processes idle by themselves. Every other Schwung module is always rendered: together they cost a few percent, and a sequencer that waits for the transport must not start late.

Performance

The first note after a rest

A rest is worth nothing if the first note after it comes late, so that is what the design was measured against: the time from a note to its first sound, awake and after a rest, taken in turn on each synth.

From the note to the first sound: the median of 14 to 30 notes each way.

For the emulated synth no time passes while it rests, so it wakes exactly where it stopped: a sound's slow LFO continues, it does not jump. One synth needed more. The emulated Virus keeps rendering a little silence ahead while it is parked, and its first note came 3.5 ms late; on waking, OsTIrus now drops exactly that silence, and never a block that sounds.

Performance

The first notes of a new sound

An emulator translates the synth's own program into the Push's the first time it runs each part of it. A sound it has not played yet brings new parts, and the first chord used to stumble while they were translated: a freshly started Vavra dropped out ten times on one chord. An emulator now knows when it is cold, from its start and from every new sound, and keeps more sound ready until the first notes are through: 44 ms instead of 7. In fourteen fresh starts of Vavra and OsTIrus, each with a sound not played before, no chord dropped out. Those first notes sound 36 ms later than the ones after them; nothing else changes. Not every synth needs it: Xenia and Nodal Red 2X never stumbled, so each emulator finds out on its first three sounds whether it does, and one that does not plays every first note at once.

Performance

Room for an emulator

Each emulator has a measured cost. One starts only while half a core would stay free with every running emulator playing at full cost, whatever they take at this moment: a resting emulator counts as a playing one, because the next note makes it one. An emulator that does not fit waits, silent, says so on its device, and starts by itself when there is room. Two run at once.

Work at low priority, such as Live indexing its library, counts as free: it yields at once.

Energy

A view that draws what has changed

The Pung view used to draw whenever anything arrived, and something always arrives: the Push says it is alive four times a second, pads send pressure, Live repeats what it has already said. Now a frame has a reason: a control turned, a value changed, or a picture is moving.

  • At rest the view checks twice a second and draws only what differs.
  • While the sound moves it draws 30 frames a second, the rate its pictures were designed for.
  • A turned knob is drawn at once.
  • While Push's own view has the screen, Pung's loop sleeps.

The same thinking runs below the view. A device where nothing happens looks for Live's changes 100 times a second instead of 500, and a note from the web page is still taken at once. An LFO of Pung's own on a silent emulator would keep it awake with every value it writes; it waits until the device can be heard again.

Performance

Sound on its own threads

The governor stands on what Pung already did for the sound. Every device renders on a realtime thread of its own, at Live's audio priority, so Pung's devices play in parallel on all four cores. That thread is plain C: nothing in Pung's view, its web page or its memory management can hold it up, and Live never waits for a module longer than half a block. Each emulator renders ahead into a buffer in its own process, so a slow moment in an emulated processor is absorbed before Live hears it.

Percent of one core, measured on a Push 3 with OsTIrus, Vavra, OB-Xd, Plaits, Dexed and Surge XT in one Set. Playing costs what it did: that is the sound.

  • A Set left open costs almost nothing. With two emulators in it, Pung went from a third of the Push's processor to a sixtieth of it.
  • More room while you play. What a silent emulator no longer takes is there for the tracks that are sounding.
  • Battery. The Push gives no reading of its battery or its power, so this page claims no hours. What it does give is how long its processor sleeps: 55% of the time with the emulators at rest, against 30% with them awake and silent, the same in two runs each. Its temperature stayed at about 65 °C in both, over the few minutes each run took and with Live indexing its library on a whole core in the background: a number that says nothing either way.

Principles

What it will not do

  • Touch anything that sounds. The governor acts only where there is nothing to hear or see. A playing Set costs what it cost before.
  • Ask you to choose. Rest is not a mode to switch on. There is nothing to choose between sound and battery, because the sound does not change.
  • Guess. Every cost it works with was measured on a Push, and every decision can be read back.
  • Make a note wait. Whatever rests is awake before the note that wakes it is played.

Proof

Measured, and tested

  • Everything is reported. GET /api/governor says what each device, emulator, thread and neighbour costs over any window. Pung's web page shows Resting beside a device and each emulator's cost on its card.
  • It is a test, not a claim. One command builds the reference Set on a Push and checks thirteen things: that the emulators rest, what silence costs, that a note into a rested synth sounds, that no block is late, how much stays free, how often the view draws. A change that costs more fails it.
  • Ten minutes of all six devices playing gave no late block and no underrun in an emulator.

Status

Status

Status

Built and measured on a Push 3 on 25 and 26 November 2026; it ships with Pung 0.4.9. Osirus rests by the same rule as JE-8086 but was not measured: it is not on the Push these figures come from. Ordinary synths still render in silence, where five of them together cost under 4 % of a core.
